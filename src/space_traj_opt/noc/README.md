# Neighborhood-Optimal Guidance from Perturbed Optimal Trajectories
## Perturbation Theory and the Method of Variations

The neighborhood-optimal guidance approach is based on a simple idea from perturbation theory and the calculus of variations: a complicated nonlinear solution can often be approximated locally by studying how the solution changes when its inputs are perturbed slightly.

Perturbation Theory

Suppose a system has a nominal solution

$$ X_0(t),\qquad U_0(t) $$

and we introduce a small perturbation to the initial condition,

$$ X_0 \rightarrow X_0+\delta X_0. $$

The resulting solution can be expressed as

$$ X=X_0+\delta X+\mathcal{O}(\|\delta X_0\|^2). $$

For sufficiently small perturbations, the higher-order terms can be neglected, giving the first-order approximation

$$ \boxed{\delta X \approx \frac{\partial X}{\partial X_0}\delta X_0}. $$

The same idea applies to the control:

$$ \boxed{\delta U \approx \frac{\partial U}{\partial X_0}\delta X_0}. $$

Thus, instead of solving the complete nonlinear problem for every possible initial condition, we can describe the behavior near a nominal solution using derivatives.

## Variations

In the calculus of variations, a variation represents a small change to a function or trajectory.

For a nominal trajectory

$$ X_0(t), $$

a neighboring trajectory is written as

$$ X(t)=X_0(t)+\epsilon\,\eta(t), $$

where \(\epsilon\) is small and \(\eta(t)\) describes the direction of the variation.

The resulting change

$$ \delta X=\epsilon\eta $$

is called the first variation.

The same concept applies to the control:

$$ U(t)=U_0(t)+\delta U(t). $$

The first variation describes how the solution responds to an infinitesimal change while ignoring second-order effects.
# Algorithm
## 1. Principle

Suppose an optimizer produces an optimal trajectory for a nominal initial state. The trajectory consists of a state history and a corresponding optimal guidance history:

$$ X_0(a)= \begin{bmatrix} x_0 & y_0 & z_0 & v_{x0} & v_{y0} & v_{z0} \end{bmatrix}^T $$

and

$$ U_0(a)= \begin{bmatrix} \theta_0(a)\\ \psi_0(a) \end{bmatrix}, $$

where \(a\) is the vehicle acceleration and is used as the independent scheduling variable.

The objective of neighborhood-optimal guidance (NOG) is to approximate the optimizer's behavior for nearby initial conditions without solving the optimization problem again during flight.

The central idea is:

Solve the optimization problem for a small neighborhood of initial conditions, then use the resulting family of optimal trajectories to construct a local linear feedback law.

## 2. The Nominal Trajectory

First solve the optimization problem for the nominal initial condition.

The result is

$$ \boxed{ X_0(a),\qquad U_0(a) } $$

where

$$ X= \begin{bmatrix} x&y&z&v_x&v_y&v_z \end{bmatrix}^T $$

and

$$ U= \begin{bmatrix} \theta\\ \psi \end{bmatrix}. $$

The acceleration \(a\) is used instead of time because the guidance is intended to be scheduled according to the vehicle's acceleration state.

Thus, the nominal solution provides:

$$ a \longrightarrow X_0(a) $$

and

$$ a \longrightarrow U_0(a). $$
## 3. Constructing the Neighborhood

The nominal trajectory describes only one initial condition.

To determine how the optimal guidance changes when the vehicle begins somewhere nearby, perturb each of the six initial state variables independently.

The six state variables are

$$ x,\quad y,\quad z,\quad v_x,\quad v_y,\quad v_z. $$

For each variable, create both a positive and negative perturbation.

This produces twelve neighboring initial conditions:

$$ X_0+\Delta x,\qquad X_0-\Delta x $$ $$ X_0+\Delta y,\qquad X_0-\Delta y $$ $$ X_0+\Delta z,\qquad X_0-\Delta z $$ $$ X_0+\Delta v_x,\qquad X_0-\Delta v_x $$ $$ X_0+\Delta v_y,\qquad X_0-\Delta v_y $$ $$ X_0+\Delta v_z,\qquad X_0-\Delta v_z. $$

Each perturbed initial condition is passed through the same trajectory optimizer.

Therefore, the optimizer produces twelve additional optimal trajectories:

$$ X_{+x}(a),\quad X_{-x}(a) $$ $$ X_{+y}(a),\quad X_{-y}(a) $$ $$ X_{+z}(a),\quad X_{-z}(a) $$ $$ X_{+v_x}(a),\quad X_{-v_x}(a) $$ $$ X_{+v_y}(a),\quad X_{-v_y}(a) $$ $$ X_{+v_z}(a),\quad X_{-v_z}(a). $$

Each trajectory also contains its own optimal pitch and yaw:

$$ U_{+x}(a),\quad U_{-x}(a),\ldots,U_{+v_z}(a),\quad U_{-v_z}(a). $$

There are therefore 13 optimized trajectories in total:
```
1 nominal
12 perturbed
```
## 4. Why the Perturbed Trajectories Are Useful

Consider the two trajectories generated by perturbing \(x\).

The optimizer gives:

$$ U_{+x}(a) $$

for the vehicle starting at \(x_0+\Delta x\), and

$$ U_{-x}(a) $$

for the vehicle starting at \(x_0-\Delta x\).

Their difference tells us how the optimal guidance changes when the initial \(x\) changes.

Using a central finite difference,

$$ \boxed{ \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \approx \frac{ U_{+x}(a)-U_{-x}(a) }{ 2\Delta x } } $$

The same calculation can be performed for every state variable.

Thus we obtain

$$ \frac{\partial U}{\partial X_0}. $$

However, this derivative is not yet the desired online feedback gain, because the state perturbation evolves as the vehicle travels along the trajectory.

The neighboring trajectories allow us to determine that relationship as well.

## 5. State Perturbations Along the Trajectory

At a particular acceleration \(a\), define the deviation of a neighboring trajectory from the nominal trajectory.

For example,

$$ \delta X_{+x}(a) = X_{+x}(a)-X_0(a) $$

and

$$ \delta X_{-x}(a) = X_{-x}(a)-X_0(a). $$

The same applies to all twelve trajectories.

For each state perturbation, form the central difference:

$$ \frac{\partial X}{\partial x_0} \approx \frac{ X_{+x}(a)-X_{-x}(a) }{ 2\Delta x }. $$

Similarly,

$$ \frac{\partial X}{\partial y_0} \approx \frac{ X_{+y}(a)-X_{-y}(a) }{ 2\Delta y } $$

and so forth.

At every acceleration \(a\), these six derivatives form a \(6\times6\) matrix:

$$ \boxed{ D_X(a) = \frac{\partial X}{\partial X_0} } $$

or explicitly,

$$ D_X(a)= \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial X}{\partial x_0} & \frac{\partial X}{\partial y_0} & \frac{\partial X}{\partial z_0} & \frac{\partial X}{\partial v_{x0}} & \frac{\partial X}{\partial v_{y0}} & \frac{\partial X}{\partial v_{z0}} \end{bmatrix}. $$

This matrix describes how the six-dimensional state neighborhood evolves around the nominal trajectory.

## 6. Guidance Perturbations

Now perform the same operation on the optimal guidance.

For the \(x\) perturbation,

$$ \frac{\partial U}{\partial x_0} \approx \frac{ U_{+x}(a)-U_{-x}(a) }{ 2\Delta x }. $$

Repeating this for all six state variables gives

$$ \boxed{ D_U(a) = \frac{\partial U}{\partial X_0} } $$

where

$$ D_U(a) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x_0} & \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial y_0} & \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z_0} & \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial v_{x0}} & \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial v_{y0}} & \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial v_{z0}} \\ \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_0} & \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y_0} & \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial z_0} & \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial v_{x0}} & \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial v_{y0}} & \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial v_{z0}} \end{bmatrix}. $$

Therefore,

$$ D_U(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times6}. $$
## 7. Relating State Error to Guidance Error

We now have two descriptions of the same neighborhood.

The state perturbation can be represented as

$$ \delta X = D_X\delta X_0 $$

and the corresponding change in optimal guidance is

$$ \delta U = D_U\delta X_0. $$

We want a direct relationship between the two:

$$ \boxed{ \delta U=K(a)\delta X } $$

where \(K(a)\) is the neighborhood-optimal feedback gain.

Substituting the two equations gives

$$ D_U\delta X_0 = K D_X\delta X_0. $$

Therefore,

$$ D_U=K D_X $$

and hence

$$ \boxed{ K(a)=D_U(a)D_X(a)^{-1}. } $$

The result is a \(2\times6\) matrix:

$$ \boxed{ K(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times6}. } $$
## 8. The Gain Is Scheduled Along the Trajectory

The calculation above is performed independently at many acceleration points.

For example,

$$ a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_N. $$

At each point we calculate

$$ K(a_1),K(a_2),\ldots,K(a_N). $$

The result is a gain table:

$$ \boxed{ K(a) } $$

rather than a single constant gain.

The table might conceptually look like:

$$ \begin{array}{c|rrrrrr|rrrrrr} a& K_{\theta x}&K_{\theta y}&K_{\theta z}&K_{\theta v_x}&K_{\theta v_y}&K_{\theta v_z}& K_{\psi x}&K_{\psi y}&K_{\psi z}&K_{\psi v_x}&K_{\psi v_y}&K_{\psi v_z} \\ \hline a_1&\cdots\\ a_2&\cdots\\ a_3&\cdots\\ \vdots\\ a_N&\cdots \end{array} $$
## 9. The Online Guidance Law

During flight, the optimizer is no longer required.

At the current acceleration \(a\), interpolate the nominal trajectory:

$$ X_0(a),\qquad U_0(a) $$

and the gain matrix:

$$ K(a). $$

Measure the actual state:

$$ X. $$

The state error relative to the nominal trajectory is

$$ \boxed{ \delta X=X-X_0(a). } $$

The neighborhood-optimal correction is then

$$ \boxed{ \delta U=K(a)\delta X. } $$

Finally,

$$ \boxed{ U=U_0(a)+K(a)\left[X-X_0(a)\right]. } $$

Expanding the two components gives

$$ \boxed{ \theta = \theta_0(a) + K_\theta(a) \left[X-X_0(a)\right] } $$

and

$$ \boxed{ \psi = \psi_0(a) + K_\psi(a) \left[X-X_0(a)\right]. } $$

The flight computer therefore performs only:
```
measure state
      ↓
measure acceleration
      ↓
look up nominal state X₀(a)
      ↓
compute δX = X - X₀(a)
      ↓
look up K(a)
      ↓
δU = K(a) δX
      ↓
U = U₀(a) + δU
      ↓
pitch / yaw command
```
## 10. No Polynomial Parameterization Is Required

An important property of this approach is that pitch and yaw do not need to be represented by polynomials.

The optimizer is free to produce whatever pitch/yaw profiles it normally produces.

The neighborhood table simply stores the resulting optimal profiles and their local sensitivities.

Thus the guidance representation is:

$$ \boxed{ \text{nominal pitch/yaw profiles} + \text{gain table }K(a) } $$

rather than

$$ \text{polynomial pitch/yaw model}. $$

This preserves the behavior of your existing optimizer.

## 11. Why the Terminal Constraints Do Not Appear in the Online Law

There is no separate terminal-condition sensitivity calculation in this formulation.

The terminal constraints are already present when each of the thirteen trajectories is optimized.

For example, if the optimizer requires every trajectory to reach a desired terminal state,

$$ X_f=X_{f,\mathrm{target}}, $$

then the \(+x\), \(-x\), \(+v_x\), etc. trajectories have all been optimized subject to that requirement.

Consequently, the information about how the optimal guidance must change to satisfy the terminal constraints is encoded in the differences between the optimized trajectories.

The NOG gain is therefore learned from:

$$ \boxed{ \text{neighboring optimal solutions} } $$

rather than explicitly derived from terminal sensitivity matrices.

## 12. Why Use ± Perturbations?

Using both positive and negative perturbations gives a central finite difference:

$$ \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \approx \frac{U_{+x}-U_{-x}}{2\Delta x}. $$

This is preferable to using only the positive perturbation:

$$ \frac{U_{+x}-U_0}{\Delta x}. $$

The symmetric perturbations also provide a useful test of the local-linear assumption.

For a sufficiently small neighborhood, we expect approximately

$$ U_{+x}-U_0 \approx -\left(U_{-x}-U_0\right). $$

If this relationship is poor, the neighborhood may be too large, the optimization may be entering a nonlinear region, or the optimal solution may be changing qualitatively.

## 13. The Complete Offline Process

The complete procedure can therefore be summarized as:

## Step 1

Solve the nominal optimization problem.

$$ \boxed{ X_0(a),U_0(a) } $$
### Step 2

Create twelve perturbed initial conditions:

$$ \boxed{ \pm\Delta x,\; \pm\Delta y,\; \pm\Delta z,\; \pm\Delta v_x,\; \pm\Delta v_y,\; \pm\Delta v_z } $$

We get something like:

```
nominal
   |
   +-- +dx
   +-- -dx
   +-- +dy
   +-- -dy
   +-- +dz
   +-- -dz
   +-- +dvx
   +-- -dvx
   +-- +dvy
   +-- -dvy
   +-- +dvz
   +-- -dvz
```

### Step 3

Solve the optimization problem independently for each perturbation.

This produces twelve neighboring optimal trajectories.

### Step 4

Interpolate all thirteen trajectories onto a common acceleration grid.

### Step 5

At each acceleration point, construct the state sensitivity matrix:

$$ \boxed{ D_X(a)=\frac{\partial X}{\partial X_0} } $$

using the central differences from the twelve trajectories.

### Step 6

Construct the optimal-guidance sensitivity matrix:

$$ \boxed{ D_U(a)=\frac{\partial U}{\partial X_0}. } $$
### Step 7

Calculate the local feedback gain:

$$ \boxed{ K(a)=D_U(a)D_X(a)^{-1}. } $$

Numerically, solve the linear system rather than explicitly calculating the inverse.

### Step 8

Store:

$$ \boxed{ \left[ a,\; X_0(a),\; U_0(a),\; K(a) \right] } $$

as your neighborhood-optimal guidance table.

Complete offline process
```
                 nominal
                     │
        ┌────────────┼────────────┐
        │            │            │
      +/-Δx..      +/-Δy      +/-Δvx ... +/-Δvz
        │            │            │
        └────────────┼────────────┘
                     ↓
              12 optimized
               trajectories
                     ↓
          interpolate onto a-grid
                     ↓
       ┌─────────────────────────┐
       │ for each acceleration a │
       │                         │
       │ DX = [δX₁ ... δX_12]    │
       │ DU = [δU₁ ... δU_12]    │
       │                         │
       │ K(a) = DU @ inv(DX)     │
       └─────────────────────────┘
                     ↓
                gain table
```
## 14. The Complete Online Process

During flight:

- Measure \(X\).
- Determine the current acceleration \(a\).
- Interpolate \(X_0(a)\).
- Interpolate \(U_0(a)\).
- Interpolate \(K(a)\).
- Calculate 
$$ \delta X=X-X_0(a). $$
- Calculate
$$ \delta U=K(a)\delta X. $$
- Command
$$ \boxed{ U=U_0(a)+\delta U. } $$

Thus a computationally expensive nonlinear trajectory optimizer is converted into a lightweight, acceleration-scheduled feedback controller.

Acceleration is used as a lookup on the gain table to extract the GainMatrix for that step:

```
acceleration
    |
    +-- nominal pitch
    +-- nominal yaw
    |
    +-- K[0,0]  d_pitch/d_x
    +-- K[0,1]  d_pitch/d_y
    +-- K[0,2]  d_pitch/d_z
    +-- K[0,3]  d_pitch/d_vx
    +-- K[0,4]  d_pitch/d_vy
    +-- K[0,5]  d_pitch/d_vz
    |
    +-- K[1,0]  d_yaw/d_x
    +-- K[1,1]  d_yaw/d_y
    +-- K[1,2]  d_yaw/d_z
    +-- K[1,3]  d_yaw/d_vx
    +-- K[1,4]  d_yaw/d_vy
    +-- K[1,5]  d_yaw/d_vz
```
The essential result is:

$$ \boxed{ \underbrace{ \text{13 optimized trajectories} }_{\text{offline}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \underbrace{ K(a) }_{\text{gain table}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \underbrace{ U=U_0(a)+K(a)\delta X }_{\text{online guidance}} } $$

This is the core neighborhood-optimal guidance construction using an offline trajectory optimizer.
